Number one. Honors gaps persist across five schools, but structure can shrink them. This Academic Medicine study looked at more than 17,000 clerkship grades from about 3,000 third-year students at five US medical schools between 2016 and 2023. Gaps in Honors by gender, race and ethnicity, and socioeconomic status persisted even after adjusting for medical knowledge. Overall Honors rates were similar in longitudinal integrated clerkships and traditional block rotations, about 40 percent versus 42 percent. But students from groups underrepresented in medicine had about 1.7 times the odds of getting Honors in the longitudinal clerkships, and so did other minority students. Grading committees didn't change overall Honors rates, but they narrowed the gap in which men got Honors less often than women. Why it matters: clerkship grades feed straight into our residency applications, and a grading committee is a fairly cheap fix to try.

Number two. Catch it early: monitoring plans go with fewer formal learning plans. This study, also in Academic Medicine, followed 470 residents in 42 programs at one Canadian institution from 2019 to 2025. Plans were coded as monitoring, minor, or major. Monitoring plans were linked to a lower chance of later minor and major plans, while minor plans did not predict major ones. Four program profiles emerged, from monitoring-heavy to intensive-plan-heavy, which points to the role of local culture. Try this: make a written, low-stakes monitoring plan a normal outcome for your clinical competency committee, not a scarlet letter. Just remember this is an association, not proof of cause.

Number three. A roadmap for AI in each of the five parts of competency-based medical education. In this open-access piece in Perspectives on Medical Education, the International Competency-Based Medical Education Collaborative maps AI onto all five core components. Examples include large language models tagging narrative comments to Milestones and EPAs, nudges when a resident has enough evidence for more autonomy, and cohort summaries that show who is getting less feedback. The authors call for AI literacy, learner involvement, and rigorous evaluation for fairness. The synthesis: it's a great menu for a competency committee or faculty retreat. Pick one component and one tool, then measure it.
