Number one, and Beth's pick. The AI wrote the discharge summary. Would you sign it? In this JAMA Network Open study at five London hospitals, 12 physicians rated about 1,300 discharge summaries against the full admission record. About half were written by clinicians, and about half by GPT-4o mini, using a prompt co-designed with clinicians. Physicians were about 22 percentage points more likely to sign off on the AI summaries with no or minor edits, and the AI drafts also rated higher on accuracy, completeness, tone, and clarity. Potential harm was rated 3 or higher on the harm scale in about 26 percent of the AI summaries, versus about 45 percent of the clinician summaries. The authors stress close clinician oversight. Why it matters: the skill shifts from writing to verifying. The invited commentary calls it a "verification paradox": the long, messy admissions where AI helps most are also the hardest to check.

Number two. Judge or coach? The paradoxes of programmatic assessment in graduate medical education. This scoping review in Medical Education looked at 47 articles on how postgraduate programs handle tensions when they adopt programmatic assessment. The tensions included structure versus flexibility, patient safety versus trainee needs, service versus training time, and supervisors balancing judging with supporting. The fixes fell into two camps: "both-and" approaches, like co-production, coaching, and shared mental models, and "either-or" approaches, like separating the assessor and coach roles. Try this: decide on purpose whether coaches also assess at your clinical competency committee, and tell residents which hat each person wears.

Number three. Beyond "be professional": how we teach conflict management. This scoping review in Medical Teacher, led by a Baylor and Texas Children's team, screened more than 2,700 citations and included 87 studies from 15 countries. Forty taught conflict management on its own, and 47 embedded it in leadership or communication curricula, often through role-play, simulation, improv, or virtual reality. Most measured only learner reactions and knowledge, and few looked at behavior change or organizational impact. Try this: add one realistic conflict case, with a consultant, a nurse, or a family, to intern simulation, then follow up with a workplace observation.
