Number one. Teach the learner-AI pair: redesigning clinical reasoning education for generative AI. In this scholarly perspective in Academic Medicine, the authors describe generative AI as an "out-in-the-world" reasoning tool. That shifts the unit of learning from the individual learner to the learner and AI working together. There's room for upskilling, but also for what they call never-skilling, mis-skilling, and deskilling. Using vignettes across training stages, they lay out five redesign strategies: introduce generative AI early, keep reasoning-first workflows, grant graduated autonomy for AI use in patient care, train faculty to supervise AI-assisted reasoning, and rethink assessment. Try this: treat AI use like any other entrustment decision on your team. Talk about when learners can use it on their own, and when they should reason first and check after.

Number two. What a longitudinal coach adds on the road to residency. This qualitative study, also in Academic Medicine, ran focus groups at Tufts in the spring and summer of 2025 with graduating students, coaches, and deans of student affairs. They found four core coaching moves: values-based reflection, emotional support, respecting student agency, and nudging students toward a broader advising network. All three groups stressed validating students and keeping them tied to their values. Why it matters: these moves map onto how we onboard interns. Asking about values on day one may pay off.

Number three. Caribbean medical schools now account for about one in twenty licensed US physicians. Using ACGME residency data from 2013 to 2023 plus national licensure data, the authors found that first US licenses to Caribbean graduates rose from 33 in 1980 to 2,768 in 2024. More than 49,000 now hold a license, about 5 percent of licensed physicians. Caribbean graduates train in family medicine at more than twice the rate of US and Canadian graduates and other international graduates, and they're younger, with an average age of about 44, compared with about 52 and 55. Why it matters: this is a big, growing primary care pipeline. The authors call for accreditation-mandated transparency on student outcomes.
