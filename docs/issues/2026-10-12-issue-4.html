Who (and what) is in the room with our learners?

Generative AI is now a reasoning partner at the bedside, so the authors of this week's top read argue we should teach and supervise the learner-AI pair, with graduated autonomy, not just the learner. Coaches who keep students tied to their values ease the jump to residency. And a growing share of the doctors our residents train beside come from Caribbean schools, most into primary care.

AIClinical reasoning

Teach the learner-AI pair: redesigning clinical reasoning education for generative AI

  • Scholarly Perspective: GAI is an "out-in-the-world" reasoning tool, so the unit of learning shifts from the individual learner to the learner-AI pair, with room for upskilling but also never-skilling, mis-skilling, and deskilling.
  • Vignettes across training stages lead to 5 redesign strategies: introduce GAI early, keep reasoning-first workflows, grant graduated autonomy for GAI use in care, train faculty to supervise GAI-assisted reasoning, and rethink assessment.
Try this: treat AI use like any other entrustment decision on your team: talk about when learners use it on their own and when they reason first and check after.
Schaye VE, Parsons AS, Olson APJ, Abdulnour RE, Daniel M. Acad Med. Online Oct 8, 2026. doi:10.1093/acamed/wvag318

UMECoaching

What a longitudinal coach adds on the road to residency

  • Qualitative focus groups at Tufts (Apr-Jul 2025) with graduating students, coaches, and deans of student affairs.
  • 4 core coaching moves: values-based reflection, emotional support, respecting student agency, and nudging students to a broader advising network. All 3 groups stressed validating students and keeping them tied to their values.
Why it matters: these moves map onto how we onboard interns. Asking about values on day 1 may pay off.
Boehm L, Blanco M, Hayes V, et al. Acad Med. Online Oct 10, 2026. doi:10.1093/acamed/wvag322

WorkforceIMGs

Caribbean medical schools: about 1 in 20 licensed US physicians

  • ACGME residency data (2013-2023) plus FSMB licensure data: first US licenses to Caribbean grads rose from 33 in 1980 to 2,768 in 2024; 49,256 now hold a license (~5% of licensed physicians).
  • Caribbean grads train in family medicine at more than twice the rate of US/Canadian grads and other IMGs, and are younger (mean age 44.1 vs 51.5 and 55.2).
Why it matters: a big, growing primary care pipeline. The authors call for accreditation-mandated transparency on student outcomes.
Gruppuso PA, Pei X, Arnhart K, et al. Acad Med. Online Oct 8, 2026. doi:10.1093/acamed/wvag317
