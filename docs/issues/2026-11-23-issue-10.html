☕ Coffee break with Beth
In my own words. Written by Beth, not AI.

A JAMA open network piece on AI discharge summaries is interesting food for thought - practicing physicians reviewed about 650 AI-generated vs about the same # of human-generated DC summaries against hospital records... and rated whether they would sign off on the summaries. Evidently the AI-generated ones were 22 percentage points more likely to be signed off on. The sticking point for educators seems to be that signing off on an AI-generated DC summary is essentially leveraging supervisory skills and judgment. I'm not sure if it's possible to instill supervisory skills earlier in our education models, or if the current structure where foundational knowledge is necessary before supervising will stand. Shifting to earlier focus on supervisory skills may end up being necessary if these agents start doing more and more?

- Beth

The AI wrote the discharge summary. Would you sign it?

  • 5 London hospitals: 12 physicians rated 1,294 discharge summaries (649 clinician-written, 645 by GPT-4o mini with a clinician-codesigned prompt) against the full admission record.
  • Signoff with no or minor edits was 22.2 points higher for LLM summaries (95% CI 19.0-27.5); noninferior. LLM drafts also rated higher on accuracy, completeness, tone, and clarity.
  • Potential harm rated 3+ in the clinical summary: 26.2% of LLM vs 45.1% of clinician summaries. Authors stress close clinician oversight.
Why it matters: the skill shifts from writing to verifying. The invited commentary calls it a "verification paradox": the long, messy admissions where AI helps most are the hardest to check.
Goldsmith K, Arribas M, Damirova F, et al. JAMA Netw Open. 2026;9(11):e2644496. Online Nov 17, 2026. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.44496

Judge or coach? The paradoxes of programmatic assessment in GME

  • Scoping review of 47 articles on how postgraduate programs handle tensions when adopting programmatic assessment.
  • Tensions: structure vs flexibility, patient safety vs trainee needs, service vs training time, and supervisors balancing judging and supporting.
  • Fixes: both/and (co-production, coaching, shared mental models) and either/or (separate assessor and coach roles).
Try this: decide on purpose whether coaches also assess at CCC, and tell residents which hat each person wears.

Beyond "be professional": how we teach conflict management

  • Baylor/Texas Children's-led scoping review: 2,716 citations screened, 87 studies from 15 countries.
  • 40 taught conflict management alone; 47 embedded it in leadership or communication curricula, often via role-play, simulation, improv, or VR.
  • Most measured only Kirkpatrick levels 1-2; few looked at behavior change or organizational impact.
Try this: add one realistic conflict case (consultant, nurse, or family) to intern simulation, then follow up with a workplace observation.
